## Q1. Under what conditions does e-value / e-process based inference stay valid when the agent picks its own actions or experiments adaptively (design depending on past data), with nuisance parameters handled by group invariance or universal inference? Known pitfalls?

### Takeaway
Validity is a theorem (Ville's inequality applied to a nonnegative (super)martingale under every null distribution). It holds at any stopping time, and under optional continuation, of the filtration in which the process is a test (super)martingale. Adaptive choice of experiments is allowed as long as each choice is predictable (fixed by information already in the filtration) and the null hypothesis says what the conditional law of the next outcome is, given the past and the chosen action. Three things break validity: (i) using a stopping or design rule that draws on a finer filtration than the one the e-process is valid in (the case for group-invariance and maximal-invariant e-processes, unless "adjusters" are used); (ii) misspecification of the null model (universal inference assumes a well-specified model); (iii) actions or covariates that carry information about the outcome noise that the filtration does not contain (feedback or non-exogenous inputs, for example closed-loop data with a wrong noise model). Also, e-values give evidence against a null. A non-rejection is not an acceptance of that null.

### Cited Findings
**Core SAVI framework**
- [THEOREM] Ramdas, Grünwald, Vovk & Shafer, "Game-Theoretic Statistics and Safe Anytime-Valid Inference", *Statistical Science* 38(4):576–601, Nov 2023 (doi:10.1214/23-STS894). SAVI provides e-processes for testing and confidence sequences for estimation that "remain valid at all stopping times". These rest on test martingales, i.e. nonnegative martingales starting at 1, which are the wealth process of a bettor. The framework is game-theoretic probability, in which successive observations are "Reality's moves". E-processes let researchers decide whether to keep collecting data without invalidating later conclusions. The paper also covers composite nulls (keywords include universal inference and reverse information projection) — [Project Euclid](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/statistical-science/volume-38/issue-4/Game-Theoretic-Statistics-and-Safe-Anytime-Valid-Inference/10.1214/23-STS894.full); [arXiv 2210.01948](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01948); [Royal Holloway PDF](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/files/58243214/Game_Theoretic_Statistics_and_Safe_Anytime_Valid_Inference_SAVI_.pdf)
- [THEOREM / conceptual] Shafer, "Testing by betting: a strategy for statistical and scientific communication", *JRSS A* 184(2):407–431, 2021 (doi:10.1111/rssa.12647). The betting score is the factor by which the money risked is multiplied, and its evidential meaning matches likelihood ratios. Testing by betting "permits opportunistic searches for significance", because the persuasiveness of having multiplied one's money by successive bets "does not depend on having followed a complete betting strategy laid out in advance". This is the conceptual basis for choosing the next bet or experiment from past results — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jorssa:v:184:y:2021:i:2:p:407-431); [PDF with discussion](https://www.glennshafer.com/assets/downloads/articles/article104_jrss_shafer-testingbybetting-with-discussion-response.pdf?lang=en-GB)
- [THEOREM] Grünwald, de Heide & Koolen, "Safe Testing", *JRSS B* 86(5):1091–1128, 2024 (doi:10.1093/jrsssb/qkae011). E-value tests stay safe (Type-I error is preserved) under optional continuation, "meaning the decision to run further tests can depend on earlier outcomes", whereas p-values do not. Growth-rate optimality (GRO) is introduced as the optional-continuation analogue of power, with worst-case (GROW) and relative (REGROW) versions. GRO e-values take the form of Bayes factors with special priors. Examples include a one-sample safe t-test and 2×2 contingency tables. In their rejoinder the authors note that Larsson, Ramdas & Ruf generalised the GRO e-variable to a "numeraire" e-variable — [CWI repository](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/34779); [arXiv 1906.07801](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.07801)
- [THEOREM] Waudby-Smith & Ramdas, "Estimating means of bounded random variables by betting", *JRSS B* 2024 (RSS discussion paper, read May 2023). Confidence intervals and sequences for bounded means are built by combining composite nonnegative (super)martingales with Ville's maximal inequality. The approach generalises and improves the Chernoff method, gives closed-form empirical-Bernstein bounds that adapt to the unknown variance, and extends to sampling without replacement. I did not find the main-article DOI; the discussion pieces are qkad112–qkad127 — [RSS discussion-meeting PDF](https://rss.org.uk/RSS/media/File-library/Events/Discussion%20meetings/rss23.pdf); [SUFE newsletter summary](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/441351)
- [THEOREM] Howard, Ramdas, McAuliffe & Sekhon, "Time-uniform, nonparametric, nonasymptotic confidence sequences", *Annals of Statistics* 49(2):1055–1080, 2021 (doi:10.1214/20-AOS1991). A confidence sequence is a sequence of intervals that is uniformly valid over an unbounded time horizon. The paper's sequences are nonasymptotic and nonparametric, and their width shrinks at about 1/√t up to log factors. The work links Cramér–Chernoff, the LIL and the SPRT, and builds on "Time-uniform Chernoff bounds via nonnegative supermartingales" (*Probability Surveys* 2020) — [Project Euclid](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-statistics/volume-49/issue-2/Time-uniform-nonparametric-nonasymptotic-confidence-sequences/10.1214/20-AOS1991.full); [Prob. Surveys](https://projecteuclid.org/euclid.ps/1589940218); [arXiv 1810.08240](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.08240.pdf)

**Adaptive actions / designs (direct evidence)**
- [THEOREM] Waudby-Smith, Wu, Ramdas, Karampatziakis & Mineiro, "Anytime-valid off-policy inference for contextual bandits" (arXiv 2210.10768, 2022, revised 2024). The confidence sequences, including doubly robust ones and CDF bands for the off-policy reward, can be used "while the original experiment is still running, when the logging policy may itself be changing due to learning, and even if the context distributions are drifting over time". They are valid at arbitrary stopping times, need only nonparametric assumptions and no known bound on importance weights, and adapt to empirical variance. This is the clearest published example of validity when actions are chosen adaptively from past data, provided the action probabilities (propensities) are known — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.10768v3); [DeepAI summary](https://api.deepai.org/publication/anytime-valid-off-policy-inference-for-contextual-bandits)

**Nuisance parameters: group invariance**
- [THEOREM] Pérez-Ortiz, Lardy, de Heide & Grünwald, "E-statistics, group invariance and anytime-valid testing", *Annals of Statistics* 52(4):1410–1432, Aug 2024 (doi:10.1214/24-AOS2394). For invariant hypotheses, the likelihood ratio of the maximal invariant is GROW among all e-statistics, invariant or not, and an anytime-valid test can be based on it. By Wijsman's representation theorem it equals a Bayes factor with a right Haar prior on the group G. Reductions by sufficiency and by invariance can be done together without losing optimality. The key condition is that G is amenable, which holds for general location and scale families and for finite-dimensional linear regression. The paper is described as an anytime-valid analogue of the Hunt–Stein theorem — [Project Euclid](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-statistics/volume-52/issue-4/E-statistics-group-invariance-and-anytime-valid-testing/10.1214/24-AOS2394.full); [arXiv 2208.07610](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07610); [CWI](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/34777)
- [THEOREM — filtration pitfall] Choe & Ramdas, "Combining Evidence Across Filtrations" (earlier title "...Using Adjusters"; arXiv 2402.09698, v5 March 2026). E-processes on the same filtration combine easily, for example by averaging. E-processes on different filtrations do not, "because their validity in a coarser filtration does not translate to validity in a finer filtration". Adjusters, which are functions that bet on the running maximum of wealth, lift an e-process from a coarser filtration to any finer one. A characterisation theorem shows that adjusters are in a sense necessary. A powerful e-process in a coarsened filtration therefore yields a powerful one in the original filtration. Applications include exchangeability and independence tests and lagged forecast comparison — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09698); [ADS abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240209698C/abstract)

**Nuisance parameters: universal inference**
- [THEOREM] Wasserman, Ramdas & Balakrishnan, "Universal inference", *PNAS* 2020. The split likelihood-ratio test and its sequential running-MLE version give finite-sample-valid tests and confidence sets "even for irregular statistical models". The guarantees require the model to be well-specified. In regular problems the method is less efficient and often conservative. (Volume and pages 117(29):16880–16890 are from memory and were not verified this session.) — [arXiv 1912.11436 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.11436v4.pdf); limitations as summarised in [Park, Balakrishnan & Wasserman 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.04034v4)
- [THEOREM] Park, Balakrishnan & Wasserman, "Robust Universal Inference For Misspecified Models" (arXiv 2307.04034, v4 Aug 2025). The target is a projection of the true distribution onto a possibly wrong model. The KL projection "can be highly non-robust to small perturbations". The robust version inverts families of robust pairwise tests and is presented as working regardless of the form of misspecification — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.04034v4)

**Feedback / closed loop pitfall (system identification)**
- [THEOREM] Forssell & Ljung, "Closed-loop identification revisited", *Automatica* 35(7):1215–1241, 1999 (doi:10.1016/S0005-1098(99)00022-9). A companion Ljung & Forssell 1999 *IEEE TAC* note states that "direct prediction error identification in closed loop may lead to biased results due to the correlation between the input and the output noise". PEM with a sufficiently flexible noise model does handle closed-loop data — [Scilit](https://www.scilit.net/publications/1d97cf9f24050978980d67a1dcb9a527); [MaRDI](https://portal.mardi4nfdi.de/wiki/Publication:1301435); [arXiv 1708.03947 citing it](https://pdf.arxiv.org/pdf/1708.03947)

### Inferences
- **Formal validity condition for adaptive designs (standard tower-property argument; not quoted from a paper).** Let F_{t-1} hold all data, all past actions and any randomisation used to pick actions. Suppose the action A_t is F_{t-1}-measurable (predictable), and the null H0 specifies the conditional density p0(y | F_{t-1}, A_t) of the next outcome Y_t. Take any predictable alternative q_t(y | F_{t-1}, A_t), such as a plug-in, a mixture or a bet. Then E_t = ∏_{s≤t} q_s(Y_s)/p0(Y_s) is a test martingale under H0, and Ville's inequality gives P_H0(∃t: E_t ≥ 1/α) ≤ α. The design (action-selection) distribution cancels: it never has to be modelled, only kept inside the filtration. This is the e-value counterpart of the likelihood principle under adaptive designs, and it matches Shafer's "no strategy laid out in advance" and the off-policy bandit results.
- **What adaptive design may NOT do.** (a) Use information outside F_t that correlates with outcome noise. Examples are an operator reacting to unlogged disturbance readings, or a slow hidden state that drives both the choice of action and the outcome while the null treats outcomes as conditionally independent of it. The null's conditional law is then wrong for the filtration actually in use, and the martingale property fails. (b) Stop or switch based on a finer filtration than the one the e-process is valid in, without an adjuster (Choe & Ramdas).
- **Slow states and autocorrelation.** If the null is "i.i.d. outcomes", a slow unmodelled state makes successive outcomes dependent, so products of per-step likelihood ratios are not test martingales and can be anti-conservative. Two fixes: (1) specify the null on one-step-ahead innovations of a dynamic model, which needs a correct, flexible noise model, as in Forssell & Ljung's closed-loop warning; or (2) randomise actions with known probabilities and use design-based, nonparametric e-processes, as in Waudby-Smith et al. and Lindon et al. (Q2).
- **Group invariance with nuisance (for example unknown noise scale σ).** The GROW e-statistic is a function of the maximal invariant, for example a t-type statistic. Its natural e-process lives in the filtration generated by the maximal-invariant sequence. Stopping and design rules should then be functions of that reduced data, or one should apply an adjuster to stay valid in the full-data filtration. This is my reading combining Pérez-Ortiz et al. with Choe & Ramdas; I could not verify the exact filtration statement in Pérez-Ortiz et al.
- **Universal inference with nuisance.** The numerator is a predictable plug-in or mixture alternative. The denominator is the null-constrained MLE at time t, which needs an optimisation over the null at each step. Validity needs no regularity, but it does need a correctly specified null family. Under misspecification, robust universal inference or a nonparametric betting construction is preferable.
- **"Acceptance".** To "accept" that an effect exists (for example a positive steady-state gain), make "no effect / effect ≤ 0" the null and reject it with an e-process. To "accept" that an effect is negligible, use an equivalence-type null (|θ| ≥ δ), or check whether a confidence sequence lies inside (−δ, δ). A non-rejected e-process is not evidence for the null.

## Q2. Sequential experiment design with e-values: anytime-valid A/B testing, adaptive designs and power

### Takeaway
Industrial anytime-valid A/B testing is mature. mSPRT "always-valid p-values" (Johari et al., Operations Research 2022) and anytime-valid linear-model / regression-adjusted tests (Lindon et al., Netflix) are both valid under continuous monitoring and data-dependent stopping. Design-based (randomisation-based) constructions also stay valid when the allocation policy adapts. "Power" is replaced by the growth rate of the e-process (GRO/GROW). The price of time-uniform validity is that confidence sequences are wider than fixed-n intervals, though their width still shrinks at about 1/√t up to log factors.

### Cited Findings
- [THEOREM] Johari, Koomen, Pekelis & Walsh, "Always Valid Inference: Continuous Monitoring of A/B Tests", *Operations Research* 70(3):1806–1821, 2022 (arXiv 1512.04922, 2015, "Bringing Sequential Analysis to A/B Testing"). They define "always valid" p-value processes that keep Type-I error controlled at any data-dependent stopping time, and show a duality between such p-values and sequential tests. Always-valid p-values derived from mixture SPRTs give an efficient power/run-time trade-off. The method was deployed on a large commercial A/B testing platform (Optimizely) — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:oropre:v:70:y:2022:i:3:p:1806-1821); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.04922); [Simons talk](https://simons.berkeley.edu/talks/always-valid-inference-continuous-monitoring-ab-tests)
- [THEOREM] Lindon, Ham, Tingley & Bojinov, "Anytime-Valid Linear Models and Regression Adjusted Causal Inference in Randomized Experiments" (arXiv 2210.08589, v5 July 2025). They give sequential F-tests and confidence sequences valid at every sample size, built on likelihood ratios of invariantly sufficient statistics, which links to group invariance. These have closed forms in terms of OLS estimates and standard errors and are GROW/REGROW optimal. They also give nonparametric, model-free sequential tests and confidence sequences for treatment effects in randomised designs, plus heteroskedasticity-robust asymptotic versions. The methods were applied to Netflix A/B tests with pre-treatment outcomes as regression adjustment — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.08589); [HBS D^3 summary](https://d3.harvard.edu/?p=24433)
- [THEOREM] Off-policy contextual-bandit confidence sequences stay valid while the logging policy learns and contexts drift (see Q1) — [Waudby-Smith et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.10768v3)
- [THEOREM / definition] GRO, the expected log-growth of the e-value under the alternative, is the optional-continuation analogue of power — [Grünwald, de Heide & Koolen](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/34779)
- [THEOREM] Confidence sequences shrink at about 1/√t up to log factors — [Howard et al. 2021](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-statistics/volume-49/issue-2/Time-uniform-nonparametric-nonasymptotic-confidence-sequences/10.1214/20-AOS1991.full)

### Inferences
- **Design for e-power.** For a simple null and alternative, the expected log-growth per step at action a is KL(q_a ‖ p0_a). An adaptive design that steers actions toward high-KL settings, such as inputs that excite the effect being tested, speeds up rejection without hurting validity, provided the choice is predictable. This follows from the GRO definition; I found no paper making this exact design argument in this session.
- **Slow effects.** If the effect only shows after a lag of several time constants, each "experiment" (hold an input, wait about kτ, measure) yields one weakly dependent observation. E-power per unit of wall-clock time therefore scales like KL/(kτ), and the expected time to reject grows linearly with τ.
- **Randomisation is a validity tool.** Randomising inputs with known probabilities (as in A/B tests and bandits) lets design-based e-processes avoid modelling the noise. This is the main protection against the feedback and non-exogeneity pitfalls of Q1.

- I could not verify the KDD 2017 version ("Peeking at A/B tests") of Johari et al., nor Ham, Imai & Janson's "design-based confidence sequences" (2022).
- I found no quantitative comparison of sample-size inflation (anytime-valid vs fixed-n) beyond the 1/√t-up-to-logs statement.

## Q3. When to stop exploring or identifying: knowledge gradient, value of information vs remaining horizon, Gittins indices, Bayesian optimal stopping; abandoning identification of an effect whose time constant exceeds the remaining horizon

### Takeaway
Optimal-learning theory says the value of information (VOI) must be weighed against the remaining exploitation horizon. Gittins indices are optimal only for infinite-horizon discounted bandits; finite horizons break the index property. The knowledge gradient (KG) is a myopic one-step look-ahead. It is optimal for a one-period horizon and in the infinite-horizon limit, but VOI is often non-concave (very noisy experiments, or many repetitions needed before learning anything), so one-step KG can wrongly value an experiment at about zero. That is exactly the situation of a slow effect that needs a long hold to identify. The clinical-trial "patient horizon" literature finds that the optimal amount of experimentation grows only like √(population or horizon). I found no source that states a rule like "abandon identification when τ exceeds the remaining horizon"; it follows from finite-horizon VOI reasoning (Inferences).

### Cited Findings
- [THEOREM] Frazier, Powell & Dayanik, "A Knowledge-Gradient Policy for Sequential Information Collection", *SIAM J. Control Optim.* 47(5):2410–2439, 2008. In Bayesian ranking and selection with independent normal arms and known common variance, KG "myopically maximizes the expected increment in the value of information" each period (a single-step Bayesian look-ahead). It is optimal when the horizon is one period and in the limit of an infinite horizon, and in some special cases for any finite horizon. Its suboptimality is bounded otherwise. The policy originates in Gupta & Miescke (1996) — [Princeton PDF](https://optimallearning.princeton.edu/Papers/Frazier%20Powell%20-%20Knowledge%20gradient%20policy.pdf); [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/a-knowledge-gradient-policy-for-sequential-information-collection/)
- [THEOREM] Frazier & Powell, "Paradoxes in Learning and the Marginal Value of Information", *Decision Analysis* 7(4):378–403, 2010 (doi:10.1287/deca.1100.0190). The marginal value of information is not concave in the number of measurements, which causes algorithmic difficulties and paradoxes. For example, with many identical alternatives it can be better to ignore some and concentrate on a few than to spread the budget evenly. VOI becomes concave once each alternative has enough measurements. The paper proposes a new fully sequential strategy (I could not verify that it is named "KG(*)"). Powell's commentary says the non-concavity is in the number of repetitions of an experiment and appears "whenever experiments are very noisy" — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ordeca/v7y2010i4p378-403.html); [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/deca.1100.0190); [Powell KG page](https://wbpowell328.github.io/sda-website/knowledgegradient/)
- [THEOREM] Gittins index policies are optimal for infinite-horizon discounted bandits. A Gittins-like index strategy is "known to be sub-optimal for a fixed horizon", and the finite-horizon optimum, found by dynamic programming, is generally not an index policy — [Gutin & Farias, "Optimistic Gittins Indices", NeurIPS 2016 metadata](https://proceedings.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2016/file/452bf208bf901322968557227b8f6efe-Metadata.json); [NeurIPS reviews](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper/2016/file/452bf208bf901322968557227b8f6efe-Reviews.html); [Optimistic Gittins Indices, Operations Research](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/epdf/10.1287/opre.2021.2207)
- [THEOREM / decision-analytic] "Patient horizon" literature: Cheng, Su & Berry (2003) treat the whole patient horizon in a fixed number of stages and choose stage sizes to maximise expected successes. Earlier decision-theoretic work includes Anscombe and Colton (1963) and Berry et al. (1994) — [review citing it, Stat. Biopharm. Res.](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/19466315.2022.2107565); [Stallard et al., Biometrical Journal (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5516263/)
- [THEOREM] Stallard et al. (Biometrical Journal, 2016/17; WRAP copy) report that in their population-size-aware Bayesian decision framework the optimal trial size is O(N^{1/2}), where N is the population (patient-horizon) size. The search summary ties this to the large-discounting regime; I did not verify the exact conditions — [PMC5516263](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5516263/); [WRAP PDF](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/81254/1/WRAP_Stallard_et_al-2016-Biometrical_Journal.pdf)
- [THEOREM / framing] Dual control: control inputs both steer the system and reveal information about it (the "dual effect", Feldbaum 1961). Mesbah, "Stochastic model predictive control with active uncertainty learning: A survey on dual control", *Annual Reviews in Control* 45:107–117, 2018 (doi:10.1016/j.arcontrol.2017.11.001), reviews MPC with active learning (probing). See also Heirung, Paulson, Lee & Mesbah, *AIChE J.* 2018, "MPC with active learning under model uncertainty: why, when, and how" — [researchr BibTeX](https://researchr.org/publication/Mesbah18-0/bibtex); [AIChE J. record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/aic.16180)

### Inferences
- **[HEURISTIC, derived] VOI with a remaining horizon H.** Roughly, VOI(experiment) ≈ P(decision changes) × (gain per unit time from the better decision) × (H − T_exp) − (cost of probing during T_exp). Here T_exp ≈ k·τ is the time to identify a lag-τ effect, with k ≈ 3–5 hold-time constants per informative step and several repetitions if noisy. When H ≤ T_exp, VOI ≤ 0 whatever the prior uncertainty, so identification of that effect should be abandoned or deferred. The agent should exploit the current certainty-equivalent decision, or randomise only within inexpensive variations.
- **Myopic KG under-values slow effects.** One short probe of a slow effect returns almost no signal, so one-step KG is about zero. This is the non-concave regime of Frazier & Powell 2010. Use multi-step look-ahead VOI, computed for the whole k·τ experiment, or KG over m repetitions (the fully sequential strategy of Frazier & Powell 2010), rather than one-step KG.
- **Gittins/KG and finite horizons.** Near the end of the horizon, exploration value goes to zero (no index policy is optimal). Exploration should be front-loaded, and the patient-horizon √N scaling suggests an exploration budget of order √(number of independent experiments that fit in the remaining horizon) ≈ √(H/(kτ)). This is an analogy, not a theorem for dynamic systems.
- **Compatibility with Q1.** Anytime-valid e-processes keep validity under ANY stopping rule, so a VOI-based stop-identification rule (Bayesian or decision-theoretic) can sit on top of an e-process or confidence sequence without inflating Type-I error. The e-process says whether the effect is established; VOI says whether further identification is worth it.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that explicitly gives a criterion like "stop identifying an effect whose time constant exceeds the remaining horizon". The rule above is derived.
- I did not verify Powell & Ryzhov, *Optimal Learning* (Wiley, 2012), or Chick & Frazier (2012, Management Science) on sequential sampling with economic stopping.
- I did not verify finite-horizon Gittins approximations (for example Bayes-UCB or finite-horizon Gittins tables).

## Q4. Patient (steady-state) vs myopic control in systems with slow state: certainty-equivalent steady-state optimisation, turnpike property, MPC horizon vs dominant time constant

### Takeaway
Turnpike theory gives the patient view [THEOREM]: for long horizons, optimal trajectories approach and stay near an optimal steady state (the turnpike) for most of the horizon. Finite-horizon economic MPC approaches infinite-horizon performance as the horizon grows, and turnpike properties are used to bound the horizon length needed for stability or near-optimality. The horizon rules are practice [HEURISTIC]: sample time at 10–20 samples per open-loop rise time; prediction horizon covering the open-loop transient or settling time (at least the dominant time constant; 2% settling is about 4τ for a first-order system); control horizon at 10–20% of the prediction horizon. Steady-state real-time optimisation (a certainty-equivalent "patient" optimiser) is limited by the steady-state wait time, which is long for slow processes. Hybrid or transient-based RTO addresses this.

### Cited Findings
- [THEOREM] Faulwasser & Grüne, "Turnpike Properties in Optimal Control" (survey of discrete- and continuous-time results; arXiv 2011.13670). In many optimal control problems, solutions for different initial conditions and horizons approach a neighbourhood of a specific steady state. Time spent near the turnpike grows with horizon length. Dissipativity-based approaches and infinite-horizon results are discussed — [arXiv 2011.13670](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13670v3)
- [THEOREM] Grüne: turnpike properties show that finite-horizon EMPC solutions converge to the infinite-horizon solution as the horizon grows, and give recursive feasibility of EMPC without terminal constraints. Pan, Stomberg, Engelmann & Faulwasser: turnpike properties "naturally enable to bound the required stabilizing horizon length" in NMPC — [Grüne, "Economic MPC without terminal constraints" (Bayreuth)](https://epub.uni-bayreuth.de/5651/1/mpceconomic_mtns.pdf); [Grüne, MPC cost design](https://epub.uni-bayreuth.de/8521/1/MPC_Cost_Design.pdf); ["First results on turnpike bounds for stabilizing horizons in NMPC" (BL record)](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100141032612_0x000001)
- [HEURISTIC] MathWorks MPC guidance: sample time with 10–20 samples within the open-loop rise time. Prediction horizon of 20–30 samples covering the open-loop transient response, so that the prediction time "should at least cover the dominant time constant" (worked example: Ts = 0.1 s, p ≈ 17–20 for a 1.7 s time constant). Prediction time "long enough to capture the dominant dynamic behavior of the plant but not any longer". Control horizon 10–20% of the prediction horizon, minimum 2–3 steps. In practice, sweep p and keep the smallest value with acceptable overshoot and settling time — [MathWorks video part 3](https://es.mathworks.com/videos/understanding-model-predictive-control-part-3-mpc-design-parameters-1530607670393.html); [MathWorks white paper](https://ch.mathworks.com/content/dam/mathworks/white-paper/3-ways-to-speed-up-model-predictive-controllers.pdf); [MathWorks run-time horizon example](https://www.mathworks.com/help/mpc/ug/evaluate-control-performance-using-run-time-horizon-adjustment.html)
- [HEURISTIC] The prediction horizon should include enough samples to cover the settling time, with settling defined as the error staying within 2% — [LGR-MPC paper, arXiv 2310.15960](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.15960). Cambridge MPC lecture notes put the sample time at about 5–10% of rise time — [Cambridge notes](https://svr-sk818-web.cl.cam.ac.uk/keshav/wiki/images/e/e4/Model_Predictive_Control.pdf)
- [Practice review] Darby et al. (2011, J. Process Control) conclude that a "fundamental limiting factor of RTO implementation is the steady-state wait-time". Steady-state detection is complex and error-prone, and the delay includes detection time plus the process settling time, which can be long for persistent disturbances. Hybrid RTO (Krishnamoorthy, Foss & Skogestad 2018) and ROPA (Matias & Le Roux 2018) use dynamic models to optimise without waiting for steady state. Feedback RTO controls the estimated steady-state gradient using transient measurements (Krishnamoorthy & Skogestad review, 2022). When disturbances vary slowly relative to plant settling time, steady-state RTO is preferred as computationally cheaper than dynamic/EMPC formulations — [Krishnamoorthy & Skogestad 2022 review PDF](https://skoge.folk.ntnu.no/publications/2022/krishnamoorthy_cce_2022_rto-as-feedback-review/RTO-as-feedback-control-review-2022.pdf); [Krishnamoorthy et al., IECR 2019](https://folk.ntnu.no/skoge/publications/2019/krishnamoorthy-feedback-RTO_IECR/acs.iecr.8b03137.pdf); [arXiv 2109.00795](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.00795); [AIChE 2016 fast steady-state estimation](https://aiche.org/conferences/aiche-annual-meeting/2016/proceeding/paper/585d-fast-estimation-plant-steady-state-application-static-rto)
- [THEOREM / framing] Certainty-equivalent control ignores the dual (probing) effect. Dual / active-learning MPC adds probing to reduce model uncertainty — [Mesbah 2018](https://researchr.org/publication/Mesbah18-0/bibtex)

### Inferences
- **[Math fact] First-order settling.** The error decays as e^{−t/τ}, so 95% is reached at 3τ (e^{−3} ≈ 0.050), 98% at about 3.9τ (ln 50), 99% at about 4.6τ and 99.3% at 5τ. "Horizon ≥ settling time ≈ 3–5τ" is therefore consistent with the MathWorks and LGR-MPC heuristics. It is a heuristic, not a theorem: turnpike theory only guarantees that suboptimality falls (often exponentially, under exponential turnpike / strict dissipativity) as the horizon grows, with constants that depend on the problem.
- **Myopic vs patient with slow state.** A horizon shorter than the slow mode's settling time cannot "see" the steady-state consequence of an action, so it trades long-run gains for short-run ones. This is the myopic failure. A long horizon (or a steady-state optimiser, i.e. certainty-equivalent RTO) is the patient policy and stays near the turnpike. Near a hard end of the operating horizon, finite-horizon optimal trajectories leave the turnpike (end effect). This is typical in turnpike descriptions but I did not verify it from the full survey.
- **Steady-state optimisation is only as good as the steady-state model.** If the slow effect's gain is unidentified (Q3), certainty-equivalent steady-state optimisation uses a prior or point estimate. Over a short remaining horizon, H < settling time, even a correct steady-state optimum cannot be reached, so the patient steady-state target matters less than the transient trajectory. Over a long remaining horizon, the turnpike argument favours investing in identification of the slow gain (dual control), because the steady-state target dominates cumulative cost.
- **Link to the RTO literature.** The steady-state wait-time problem is the operational form of "the time constant is long relative to the decision cadence". Transient-based gradient estimation (feedback RTO, hybrid RTO) is the practical route to patient optimisation without waiting for full settling.
