# Bandits & RL with delayed / carryover / long-term effects and switching costs — implications for a 365-step horizon with hidden memory (time constant 30–120 steps)

Method note: in this session, arxiv.org and proceedings.mlr.press could not be fetched (network restriction), so findings are taken from search-engine extractions of abstracts, proceedings pages and paper text. Exact constants are therefore stated only where a source gave them. Tags: **[Thm]** = proven theorem/bound; **[Emp]** = empirical/simulation/A-B finding; **[Model]** = definition/setting. All numerical evaluations at T = 365 are my own arithmetic (in Inferences), not from the sources.

Reference arithmetic used below (my computation): √365 ≈ 19.1; 365^(1/3) ≈ 7.1; 365^(2/3) ≈ 51; 365^(3/4) ≈ 83; ln 365 ≈ 5.9; ln ln 365 ≈ 1.8. For a first-order lag with time constant τ, the share of the new steady state reached t steps after a switch is 1 − e^(−t/τ): 63% at t = τ, 86% at 2τ, 95% at 3τ. For τ = 30 that means 95% at about 90 steps. For τ = 120 it means 95% at about 360 steps, nearly the whole horizon.

## Q1 — What do regret bounds for delayed/aggregated feedback, carryover/memory bandits (recovering, recharging, rotting, rising, blocking) and switching costs imply for T ≈ 365 and memory ≈ 30–120?

### Takeaway
The theory is clear on the shape of the costs. With **pure delay** (the reward is real but arrives late), regret grows by an **additive** term of about K·E[delay] in the stochastic case and **multiplicatively** in the adversarial case. When **rewards are spread over d steps and mixed together** (anonymous composite feedback), the rate is Θ̃(√(dKT)). **Switching costs or an adaptive environment with memory** push the rate to Θ̃(T^(2/3)), and the matching algorithms work by **holding arms in blocks**. At T = 365 with memory 30–120, almost every worst-case bound is a large fraction of T or exceeds it (vacuous). So the bounds point in the right direction (hold actions in blocks, expect few informative experiments), but they cannot set the size of the exploration budget. That has to come from an explicit finite-horizon Bayesian value-of-information calculation.

### Cited Findings
**Delayed feedback (reward attributable, but late)**
- [Thm] Joulani, György & Szepesvári (ICML 2013, PMLR 28:1453–1461), "Online Learning under Delayed Feedback": delay increases regret **multiplicatively in adversarial problems and additively in stochastic problems**. They give black-box meta-algorithms that turn non-delayed algorithms into delay-robust ones, plus delayed-UCB variants with lower complexity. — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v28/joulani13.html); [arXiv 1306.0686](https://arxiv.org/abs/1306.0686)
- [Thm] Pike-Burke, Agrawal, Szepesvári & Grünewälder (ICML 2018, PMLR 80:4105–4113), "Bandits with Delayed, Aggregated Anonymous Feedback". Setting: the learner sees only the **sum** of rewards arriving in each round, so it does not know which arm generated what. Result: the **additive** delay penalty survives anonymity and aggregation, provided E[τ] (or a bound on it) is known. The ODAAF algorithm matches the non-anonymous worst case exactly for bounded delays, and up to logs or an additive variance term for unbounded delays. The paper's comparison table gives roughly O(√(KT log K) + K·E[τ]) (the square root was garbled in extraction; check the PDF). — [PMLR page](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v80/pike-burke18a.html); [arXiv 1709.06853](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.06853); [ICML 2018 poster](https://icml.cc/virtual/2018/poster/2212)
- [Thm] Cesa-Bianchi, Gentile & Mansour (COLT 2018; JMLR 23(277), 2022, with Cesari & Colomboni), "Nonstochastic Bandits with Composite Anonymous Feedback". The loss of an action is **spread over the next d rounds** and only the sum is observed, which is the closest formal model to a "carryover" lever. A reduction gives regret of order **√(dKT)** (ignoring logs), with a **matching lower bound**. By comparison, non-anonymous delayed feedback has rate √((d+K)T), so anonymity plus spreading is strictly harder. — [PMLR v75](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v75/cesa-bianchi18a.html); [JMLR](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v23/21-1443.html); [draft PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/162979412.pdf)

**Switching costs / adaptive (memory) adversaries**
- [Thm] Dekel, Ding, Koren & Peres (STOC 2014), "Bandits with Switching Costs: T^(2/3) Regret". With unit switching cost, minimax regret is **Θ̃(T^(2/3))**, with lower bound Ω̃(K^(1/3)T^(2/3)) for K ≤ T. Without switching costs the rate is Θ(√(KT)). With full information plus switching costs it is Θ(√(T log K)), so bandit feedback is strictly harder here. The **same rate applies to bandits against bounded-memory adaptive adversaries and to adversarial MDPs**. Proof technique: a multi-scale random walk. — [MSR PDF](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/DekelDiKoPe14.pdf); [MSR page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=327647); [arXiv 1310.2997](https://arxiv.org/abs/1310.2997v2)
- [Thm] Arora, Dekel & Tewari (ICML 2012), "Online Bandit Learning against an Adaptive Adversary: from Regret to Policy Regret". Against an adversary with **unbounded memory, no bandit algorithm achieves sublinear policy regret**. With **bounded memory**, **mini-batching** (repeating each chosen action over a batch) turns an O(T^q)-regret algorithm into O(T^(1/(2−q))) policy regret. That gives T^(2/3) for K-armed bandits, T^(3/4) for bandit linear/submodular optimization and T^(4/5) for bandit convex optimization. — [arXiv 1206.6400](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1206.6400.pdf); [MSR page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=327776)
- [Thm] Anava, Hazan & Mannor (NIPS 2015), "Online Learning for Adversaries with Memory: Price of Past Mistakes". They extend learning-with-memory to online convex optimization, with algorithms that achieve optimal regret for convex and strongly convex losses. The paper notes that earlier blocking techniques (Merhav et al.) gave O(T^(2/3)) policy regret against bounded-memory adversaries. — [NeurIPS proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2015/hash/38913e1d6a7b94cb0f55994f679f5956-Abstract.html); [PDF](https://proceedings.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2015/file/38913e1d6a7b94cb0f55994f679f5956-Paper.pdf)

**Reward depends on time since the arm was last played (recovering / recharging / delay-dependent)**
- [Model+Thm] Pike-Burke & Grünewälder (NeurIPS 2019, spotlight), "Recovering Bandits". Each arm's expected reward is an unknown function of **time since it was last played**, modelled with a Gaussian process. RGP-UCB and RGP-TS use GP posteriors to **look ahead d steps** and plan sequences. The paper argues that **lookahead regret**, not instantaneous regret, is the right criterion, and gives an optimistic-planning approximation to cut computation. A third-party summary reports Bayesian regret of about Õ(√(KdT)) for certain kernels (and "O*(√KT)"); I could not confirm the exact d-dependence from primary text. — [NeurIPS abstract](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2019/hash/9a093d729036a5bd4736e03c5d634501-Abstract.html); [paper PDF](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/9a093d729036a5bd4736e03c5d634501-Paper.pdf); [arXiv 1910.14354](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/1910.14354)
- [Model+Thm] Kleinberg & Immorlica (FOCS 2018, pp. 309–319), "Recharging Bandits": an arm's expected payout is an **increasing concave function of time since last played**. A later paper reports that it gives an asymptotic (1−ε)-approximation for the planning problem under weak concavity. A related talk describes a PTAS for planning that is then used to get sublinear regret. I did not retrieve the exact regret bound. — [dblp](https://dblp.org/rec/html/conf/focs/KleinbergI18); [MSR page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=478956); [Papadigenopoulos & Caramanis follow-up](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.14790); [UCI seminar](https://ics.uci.edu/~theory/269/190201a.html)
- [Thm] Cella & Cesa-Bianchi (AISTATS 2020, PMLR 108:1168–1177), "Stochastic Bandits with Delay-Dependent Payoffs": expected reward depends on rounds since last pull. **Finding the optimal policy is NP-hard even with known parameters.** Ranking policies approximate the optimum to within a constant factor. Regret relative to the best ranking policy is about √(kT) (plus lower-order terms), and the algorithm makes only **O(k ln ln T) switches**. — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v108/cella20a.html); [arXiv 1910.02757](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.02757)
- [Thm] Basu, Sen, Sanghavi & Shakkottai (NeurIPS 2019), "Blocking Bandits": an arm becomes unavailable for a fixed delay after it is played. Oracle Greedy is (1 − 1/e − O(1/T))-optimal. UCB-Greedy has regret O(K(K−K*)/Δ log T), with a matching-form lower bound Ω((K−K*)/Δ log T). The authors note that **blocking produces "free exploration"**. — [NeurIPS page](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/8725-blocking-bandits); [arXiv 1907.11975](https://arxiv.org/html/1907.11975v2)

**Rewards that decay or rise with use (rotting / rising / memory windows)**
- [Thm] Levine, Crammer & Mannor (NIPS 2017), "Rotting Bandits": expected reward of an arm **decreases each time it is pulled**, so the optimal policy alternates arms. Regret is Õ(K^(1/3)T^(2/3)) (as summarized by Seznec et al.). — [NeurIPS](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2017/hash/97d98119037c5b8a9663cb21fb8ebf47-Abstract.html)
- [Thm] Seznec, Locatelli, Carpentier, Lazaric & Valko (AISTATS 2019), "Rotting bandits are not harder than stochastic ones". FEWA achieves O(log(KT)) problem-dependent and O(√(KT)) problem-independent regret, assuming bounded decays. — [PMLR v89](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v89/seznec19a.html); [arXiv 1811.11043](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1811.11043)
- [Thm] Heidari, Kearns & Roth (IJCAI 2016), "Tight Policy Regret Bounds for Improving and Decaying Bandits". **Decreasing** rewards: a greedy policy has **constant** policy regret, bounded in the number of arms. **Increasing concave** rewards: sublinear policy regret that depends on the time needed to tell the best arm from the rest. External regret can be zero while policy regret is large. — [IJCAI abstract](https://www.ijcai.org/Abstract/16/224); [PDF](https://cis.upenn.edu/~aaroth/Papers/decayingbandits.pdf)
- [Thm] Metelli, Trovò, Pirola & Restelli (ICML 2022, PMLR 162:15421–15457), "Stochastic Rising Bandits": arms have non-decreasing expected payoffs (rested and restless versions). R-ed-UCB and R-less-UCB have instance-dependent regret, and **Õ(T^(2/3))** under some conditions. — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/metelli22a.html); [ICML](https://icml.cc/virtual/2022/spotlight/16244)
- [Thm] Clerici, Laforgue & Cesa-Bianchi (TMLR 2024), "Linear Bandits with Memory: from Rotting to Rising". Reward depends on past actions within a **window of size m**, with an exponent γ for rotting (γ<0) or rising (γ>0). **Computing the optimal action sequence is NP-hard**, so they compete with **cyclic policies**. The OFUL variant has regret Õ(√d·(m+1)^(1/2+max{γ,0})·T^(3/4)), with cycle length tuned to trade approximation against estimation. Unknown m and γ are handled by bandit model selection. — [arXiv 2302.08345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08345v2); [TMLR listing](https://mlanthology.org/tmlr/2024/clerici2024tmlr-linear)

### Inferences
- **Numbers at T = 365 (my arithmetic, rewards normalized to [0,1] per step, so regret is in "steps of maximum reward"):**
  - Switching-cost lower bound Ω̃(K^(1/3)T^(2/3)): about 64 for K=2 and 87 for K=5, i.e. 18–24% of the horizon, before constants and logs.
  - Composite-anonymous √(dKT) with d = 30, K = 2: about 148 (41% of T). With d = 120, K = 2: about 296 (81%). With d = 120, K = 5: about 468 (more than T, vacuous).
  - Additive delay penalty K·E[τ] with K = 3, E[τ] = 60: about 180 (49% of T).
  - Linear-bandits-with-memory bound √d(m+1)^(1/2)T^(3/4) with d = 1, γ = 0: about 465 at m = 30 and about 915 at m = 120. Both are vacuous.
  - Worst-case theory therefore says that **learning a carryover lever with τ = 30–120 inside 365 steps is borderline possible at τ ≈ 30 and essentially impossible at τ ≈ 120**, unless the agent has strong priors or fast surrogates.
- **Number of "independent looks" is the real budget.** With memory τ, the horizon holds about T/τ ≈ 12 (τ=30) down to ≈ 3 (τ=120) blocks of length τ. If each plateau must last about 2τ to show ~86% of its steady-state effect, that drops to about 6 or about 1.5 informative plateaus. That is a few-armed, few-sample regime where constants and priors dominate asymptotic rates.
- **Mapping to the simulated levers:** price with hidden word-of-mouth memory ≈ composite/anonymous delayed feedback with a rising/rotting memory window (Cesa-Bianchi et al.; Clerici et al.). Maintenance with hidden wear ≈ recovering/recharging bandits, where payoff depends on time since the last maintenance (Pike-Burke & Grünewälder; Kleinberg & Immorlica; Cella & Cesa-Bianchi). The NP-hardness results suggest restricting to simple **cyclic or periodic policies** (e.g., maintain every k steps, or hold price p for L steps), which is how Clerici et al. and Cella & Cesa-Bianchi get tractable guarantees.
- **The "free exploration" and greedy results** (blocking bandits; decaying bandits with constant greedy regret) suggest that in pure *decay* settings (e.g., wear) a greedy/myopic rule can be close to optimal. The hard cases are *rising/carryover* effects, where a payoff appears only after the action is held. Word-of-mouth after a price cut is exactly that case.

### Gaps
- Exact constants and d-dependence of the recovering-bandits Bayesian regret, and the exact regret bound of Kleinberg & Immorlica, could not be checked against primary text (arXiv and PMLR were unreachable).
- I found no paper that states regret bounds as an explicit function of an **exponential time constant** τ (as opposed to a hard window m or delay d). Treating τ ≈ m is my approximation.
- No source found gives finite-sample (T ≈ 300–400) numerical regret for these algorithms. Published experiments typically use T in the thousands or more.

## Q2 — Batched / blocked policies: does holding an arm for blocks of length ≈ memory help?

### Takeaway
Yes, both in theory and in design practice. Mini-batching or blocking is the standard way to get sublinear policy regret against memory and to control switching costs. Very few batches are needed for near-minimax rates: Θ(log log T), i.e. about 2–3 at T = 365. Switching-budget results show regret depends on the number of allowed switches in discrete phases. Caveat: the theory's block length (≈ T^(1/3) ≈ 7 for constant memory) assumes memory much shorter than the block. With memory 30–120 the block must be at least the memory length, and that caps the number of blocks at roughly 3–12.

### Cited Findings
- [Thm] Perchet, Rigollet, Chassang & Snowberg (Annals of Statistics 44(2):660–681, 2016; COLT 2015), "Batched Bandit Problems" (two-armed stochastic). With **M = Θ(log log T) batches** the minimax-optimal regret (up to logs), O(√T·log log log T), is achieved. With M = Θ(log(T/log T)) batches the adaptive rate log(TΔ²)/Δ is achieved. — [Project Euclid](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-statistics/volume-44/issue-2/Batched-bandit-problems/10.1214/15-AOS1381.full); [MIT open access](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/98879)
- [Thm] Arora, Dekel & Tewari (ICML 2012): **mini-batching** (holding each action for a block) is the mechanism that turns O(T^q) regret into O(T^(1/(2−q))) **policy regret** against bounded-memory adaptive adversaries. With unbounded memory, sublinear policy regret is impossible. — [arXiv 1206.6400](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1206.6400.pdf)
- [Thm] Dekel et al. (STOC 2014): the T^(2/3) rate achieved by blocking is **tight** for switching costs and bounded-memory adversaries, so in the worst case blocking cannot be beaten. — [MSR PDF](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/DekelDiKoPe14.pdf)
- [Thm] Simchi-Levi & Xu (NeurIPS 2019; Management Science 69(12):7182–7201, 2023), "Phase Transitions (and Cyclic Phenomena) in Bandits with Switching Constraints". Stochastic MAB with a hard budget S on total switching cost, with matching upper and lower bounds. For fixed K, optimal regret shows **phase transitions**: equal-length phases in S within which the rate barely changes, separated by abrupt drops. General switching costs link the problem to the shortest Hamiltonian path problem. — [NeurIPS abstract](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2019/hash/ae31ee951b4d4bfb5518e0fcdc064a83-Abstract.html); [RePEc (MS 2023)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v69y2023i12p7182-7201.html)
- [Thm] Cella & Cesa-Bianchi (AISTATS 2020): in delay-dependent payoff bandits, near-optimal regret relative to ranking policies is achieved with only **O(k ln ln T) switches**. — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v108/cella20a.html)
- [Thm] Clerici, Laforgue & Cesa-Bianchi (TMLR 2024): with window-m memory, regret is controlled by playing **cyclic policies whose cycle length is tuned** to trade approximation error against estimation error. — [arXiv 2302.08345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08345v2)
- [Thm] Garivier, Lattimore & Kaufmann (NeurIPS 2016), "On Explore-Then-Commit Strategies" (two Gaussian arms): ETC (a single exploration block, then commit) is **asymptotically suboptimal by a factor of 2** relative to fully sequential strategies. Fixed-design ETC is about 4 log(T)/Δ. SPRT-style stopping improves on that but stays suboptimal, and a lower bound shows no ETC can do better. Extraction of the constants was garbled; verify before quoting. — [NeurIPS PDF](https://proceedings.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2016/file/ef575e8837d065a1683c022d2077d342-Paper.pdf); [NeurIPS page](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/6179-on-explore-then-commit-strategies)
- [Thm/design] Hu & Wager (arXiv 2209.00197), "Switchback Experiments under Geometric Mixing" (MDP with bounded mixing). For the global average treatment effect with regular Bernoulli switchbacks, the **optimal block length scales as T^(1/3)** and RMSE decays as T^(−1/3), versus T^(−1/2) without carryover. Judicious **burn-in** (discarding observations within b steps after a switch) can reach about log(T)^(1/2)·T^(−1/2). Elsewhere they argue that b = 0 with long blocks is rate-optimal unless burn-in bias is negligible. The two claims concern different estimators and regimes. — [arXiv HTML v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2209.00197v4); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/arxpapers/2209.00197.htm)

### Inferences
- **Block length vs. memory at T = 365.** Theory sets block length by trading the contamination cost per switch (≈ memory) against the information cost of fewer switches. For constant memory the optimum is about T^(1/3) ≈ 7 steps. With memory τ = 30–120 the contamination per switch is so large that blocks should be **at least τ, ideally 2–3τ**. That leaves about 2–6 plateaus (τ=30) or 1–2 (τ=120) over the year.
- **Very few switches are needed.** log log 365 ≈ 1.8, so Perchet et al.'s result says 2–3 adaptive batches already give near-minimax performance. Cella & Cesa-Bianchi's O(k ln ln T) switches for k = 3 is about 5. This supports a **"few deliberate long plateaus"** design over frequent probing.
- **ETC is cheap to implement and only modestly suboptimal** (factor ~2 asymptotically). In a regime with 1–6 informative plateaus, a Bayesian "explore one long plateau early, then commit/adapt" plan is a reasonable baseline. Fully sequential adaptivity has little room to pay off when only a handful of decisions are possible.
- **Burn-in is the operational form of "wait for the plateau".** Score a plateau only on its second half (or after about τ steps). Hu & Wager suggest this mainly helps bias, and that very long blocks without burn-in can be equally good for some estimands.
- **Bandits with Knapsacks** (named in the question) is about budget/resource constraints, not carryover. I found nothing linking it to holding arms for memory-length blocks, and I did not research it further.

### Gaps
- The exact phase-transition formula of Simchi-Levi & Xu (regret as a function of switching budget S and K) was not retrieved. **Unverified recollection**: regret ≈ Θ̃(T^(1/(2−2^(−q(S))))) with q(S) = ⌊(S−1)/(K−1)⌋. Check the paper before using it.
- Whether "block ≈ 2–3τ" is optimal for an exponential (not hard-window) carryover over a 365-step horizon is not established in any source I found. It is my inference.

## Q3 — Long-term vs short-term metrics: surrogate indices and switchback design with carryover (optimal switching interval relative to carryover length)

### Takeaway
When the long-term effect is slow, two families of tools apply. (a) **Surrogates/progressive feedback**: combine fast leading indicators into a predicted long-term outcome, so learning need not wait out the full lag. (b) **Switchback designs whose epochs are matched to the carryover length m.** Bojinov–Simchi-Levi–Zhao derive minimax-optimal designs as a function of the carryover order and show that inference stays valid, with a modest variance cost, under misspecification. Hu–Wager show block lengths must grow with the horizon (T^(1/3)) under Markovian carryover.

### Cited Findings
- [Thm] Bojinov, Simchi-Levi & Zhao (Management Science 69(7):3759–3777, 2023; DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2022.4583), "Design and Analysis of Switchback Experiments". The optimal switchback design is cast as a **minimax discrete robust optimization**: identify the worst-case adversary, derive structural results, solve via continuous relaxation. Optimal designs are derived **under different assumptions on the order m of the carryover effect**. Inference uses exact randomization p-values and a new finite-population CLT. The paper also gives results for a **misspecified carryover order** (early version: estimation and inference remain valid with a minor variance increase) and a **data-driven procedure to identify m**. — [arXiv 2009.00148](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.00148v2); [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/epdf/10.1287/mnsc.2022.4583); [HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/design-and-analysis-of-switchback-experiments)
- [Thm] Hu & Wager (arXiv 2209.00197): under geometric mixing, standard switchbacks have carryover bias and error ~T^(−1/3). The optimal regular block length is about T^(1/3), and burn-ins can improve error to about log(T)^(1/2)T^(−1/2). — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2209.00197v4)
- [Thm/Emp] Athey, Chetty, Imbens & Kang (NBER WP 26463, 2019), "The Surrogate Index: Combining Short-Term Proxies to Estimate Long-Term Treatment Effects More Rapidly and Precisely". The surrogate index is the predicted long-term outcome given several short-term outcomes. The treatment effect on the index equals the long-term effect if the long-term outcome is independent of treatment conditional on the index (surrogacy). They characterize bias under violations and give validation tests. **[Emp]** In a multi-site California job-training experiment, long-term employment effects could have been estimated from the **first 6 quarters**, with **35% smaller standard errors**. — [NBER PDF](https://nber.org/papers/w26463.pdf); [Opportunity Insights](https://opportunityinsights.org/paper/the-surrogate-index/)
- [Emp] McDonald, Maystre, Lalmas, Russo & Ciosek (KDD 2023), "Impatient Bandits: Optimizing Recommendations for the Long-Term Without Delay". A **Bayesian filter** combines partial, progressively revealed outcomes and short-term surrogates into a belief over the long-term (about 2-month) reward, inside a bandit (Thompson-style) policy. In Spotify podcast experiments it beat both waiting for the delayed reward and using a day-2 proxy, came closer to the oracle, and won an A/B test at very large scale. — [arXiv 2307.09943](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.09943); [author page](https://tomcdonald.github.io/publication/mcdonald2023impatient)
- [Thm] Zhang, Baldwin-McDonald, Ciosek, Maystre & Russo (arXiv 2501.07761, 2025), "Impatient Bandits: Optimizing for the Long-Term Without Delay". Formal "bandits with progressive feedback" model with regret analysis, and a **"Value of Progressive Feedback"** metric that measures how informative leading indicators observed before the final outcome are. — [arXiv 2501.07761](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.07761v1)

### Inferences
- **Switching interval ≈ carryover length is the design rule.** Bojinov et al. optimize the design with respect to m, Hu & Wager require blocks longer than mixing, and Arora et al. block to neutralize memory. For τ = 30–120 that means plateaus of at least 30–120 steps, so a 365-step run is roughly a 3–12-period switchback at most.
- **Misspecification:** Bojinov et al. report that inference stays valid under a misspecified m at a variance cost, which argues for using a **conservative (longer) plateau** when τ is uncertain. The asymmetry is my reasoning: too-short plateaus bias the estimate, too-long ones only lose samples. Confirm against the paper's misspecification theorem.
- **Surrogates are the main lever for beating the τ bottleneck.** If the simulated business exposes leading indicators (e.g., new-customer inflow, reviews, defect/breakdown precursors, wear sensor proxies), a surrogate index or progressive Bayesian filter can cut the effective delay well below τ. The Athey et al. and Spotify results are empirical evidence that such gains (35% SE reduction; outperforming proxy-only and delay-only) are achievable. They are not guarantees for a simulated environment.
- A Bayesian agent should **model the lag explicitly** (e.g., a first-order state with unknown τ) and update on partial trajectories within a plateau. That is the "progressive feedback" idea, which extracts information before steady state is reached.

### Gaps
- **Unverified recollection (not confirmed this session):** for known m and T = n·m, the Bojinov et al. minimax-optimal design randomizes at periods {1, 2m+1, 3m+1, …, (n−2)m+1}, i.e. epochs of length m with longer (≈2m) first and last epochs. Check Section 4 of the paper before relying on it.
- I could not retrieve the direction of misspecification effects (over- vs under-estimating m) from primary text.
- No source directly addresses switchbacks where the outcome is **profit to be maximized during the experiment** (regret) rather than estimation error. Switchback theory optimizes estimation, not earn-while-learning.

## Q4 — RL with slow state: cost of exploration when effective horizon ≈ time constant; myopic under-exploration; value of information shrinking with remaining horizon

### Takeaway
Two results from operations-research pricing apply directly. **Myopic (certainty-equivalent / greedy-Bayesian) policies can stop learning prematurely ("incomplete learning")**, and modest forced-exploration fixes ("constrained" or "semi-myopic" policies) restore near-optimal regret. When demand has **memory (reference-price effects)**, asymptotically optimal policies change prices **slowly** to control the hidden state while learning. With a finite undiscounted horizon, Bayes-optimality of the Gittins index is lost and the value of exploration depends on the remaining horizon. Finite-horizon Gittins is a tractable, near-optimal heuristic. With τ = 30–120 and T = 365, the effective horizon is only 3–12 time constants, so exploration must be front-loaded or skipped.

### Cited Findings

The proposed policy is **asymptotically optimal** (regret grows at the smallest possible rate in T) under various reference-price updating mechanisms, and **changes prices very slowly to control reference-price evolution while gradually accumulating data**. ### Inferences
- **Effective horizon in time constants:** H_eff = T/τ ≈ 12 (τ=30), 6 (τ=60), 3 (τ=120). An exploration plateau "pays" only if, after it ends, there remain about τ steps to move the state to the new optimum plus enough steps to collect the gain. Rough rule (my derivation): information gathered at time t is worth approximately (gain per step) × max(0, T − t − τ). So **value of information falls linearly with remaining horizon and hits zero about τ steps before the end**. For τ = 120, any experiment that has not concluded by step ≈ 245 (365 − 120) cannot pay back through the slow channel.
- **Front-load or forgo:** for τ ≈ 30, one or two plateaus of 60–90 steps in the first ~150 steps leave about 200+ steps to exploit. For τ ≈ 120, a single 2τ = 240-step plateau (≈86% of steady state) leaves about 125 steps. That is often too little to recover the exploration cost unless the prior gap is large. A Bayesian agent should compute this explicitly (finite-horizon Gittins / lookahead) rather than rely on regret rates.
- **Myopia pitfall:** a myopic Bayesian agent that judges a price cut after a few steps will see mostly the immediate margin loss and not the slow word-of-mouth gain. It will under-explore, which is the Harrison–Keskin–Zeevi incomplete-learning failure plus Heidari et al.'s "improving arms" difficulty. Constrained/semi-myopic rules (forced minimum variation, minimum plateau length) are the theoretically backed fix.
- **For hidden wear (maintenance):** decaying-reward theory (greedy near-optimal) and recovering-bandit lookahead suggest a periodic maintenance policy whose interval is learned from the recovery curve. Deliberate exploration there is less valuable than for rising/word-of-mouth effects.
- **For hidden word-of-mouth memory (price):** den Boer & Keskin's "move prices slowly" result says that, with a memory state, the optimal learning policy is itself slow and smooth. That supports maintained plateaus or gradual ramps over rapid A/B toggling.
